Section I · Why the clock turns
1

The 364-day mystery

A solar year lasts about 365.24 days, so a calendar that wants to stay with the seasons has to account for the extra quarter day. The Gregorian calendar, the one we all use, does it by adding: a leap day every four years, with further rules at the century marks, so that over long spans the calendar and the sun stay together.

The Enoch calendar adds nothing. Its year is 364 days, exactly 52 weeks, and it never changes. That is the mystery: how can a year that is fixed, and 1.24 days shorter than the sun’s, stay with the seasons?

2

Adding or removing days is strictly forbidden

One principle of the Enoch clock is not negotiable: days are never added and never removed.

Scholars have long tried to reconcile that with the sun anyway, proposing intercalations, which are days or weeks inserted to pull a calendar back into line. The best of these is the 294-year cycle found among the Qumran scrolls (4Q319), which says “the year completes its cycle.” The arithmetic is striking: 294 years of 364 days is 107,016 days, and that equals 293 solar years to within 55 minutes.

But look at what happens inside that cycle. The calendar moves away from the seasons by 1.24 days every year, until at year 147 it is half a year out and the clock says summer while the sky says winter. A calendar that is out of alignment with the seasons for 294 years is not much use to anyone.

3

The 294 year, 294 week, 294 day key

The 294-year cycle brings the calendar back into step, but only once in 294 years. How often should the correction come? The answer is in the same number.

The sun gains a day on the calendar about every 294 days, and the number comes back at two larger scales. Over 294 years the sun gains a whole year: the cycle known from Qumran. Over 294 weeks, about 5.65 years, it gains a whole week, a scale no one had noticed. Both are far longer than a year, and the text asks the calendar to keep pace every year with perfect justice:

And the sun and the stars bring in all the years exactly, so that they do not advance or delay their position by a single day unto eternity; but complete the years with perfect justice in 364 days.

1 Enoch 74:12

Only the 294-day cycle, which is 42 weeks, is shorter than a year, and it fits inside the other two: 294 days × 7 is 294 weeks, and 294 weeks × 52 is 294 years.

So the correction comes every 294 days, not every 294 years and not every 294 weeks. The next question is what that correction does to the calendar, which is forbidden to add or remove a day.

4

The shift: one day back, every 294 days

Start the calendar on the equinox. The sun needs 365.24 days to come back to it and the Enoch year is 364, so Day 1 of the next year arrives 1.24 days early, and another 1.24 days earlier with every year that follows.

It is not an intercalation rule. It is a shift. When the sun slips out of alignment, the clock is rotated back into line with it, and nothing is interrupted: the days, weeks, months and years carry on as before. Every other calendar answers drift by changing the count, adding or dropping days, and for centuries scholars tried the same road with the Enoch calendar and found nothing that worked. They called it “hopelessly primitive” and “astronomically unworkable” (Stern 2001). But Enoch forbids that road, and so it forces a different solution: leave the calendar alone and turn it against the sun. It looks simple in hindsight, and interestingly, of every known calendar that keeps pace with the seasons, not one does it this way: they add or remove days, or they align through observation.

Section II · Reading the clock
5

Sunrise starts the day, spring starts the year

Now that you know why the clock turns, let’s learn to read it.

The Gregorian calendar starts its day in the middle of the night and its year in the middle of winter. The Enoch clock starts its day at sunrise and its year in spring, at the equinox.

In this way he rises in the first month in the great portal, which is the fourth.

1 Enoch 72:6

Press 1 for the Sky to watch that sunrise.

6

The pendulum swing of the sun

Hold the time at the first part of the day, the sunrise, and step through the days. The sun never rises in the same place twice.

Its rising point swings along the horizon like a pendulum: north toward summer, back through due east at the equinox, south toward winter, and back again. Like a pendulum, it moves fastest through the middle and slows at each end, where it turns around. The turning points are the solstices.

You can make the same swing visible in the app. Choose Day in the playback bar, hold the time at the first part of the day, and press the right arrow or play.

7

What are the portals?

The portals are the measure the sun makes, with the months as the dividers.

The sun’s rising point moves a little each morning, and the month borders cut its path into portals. The months divide the horizon, and they size the portals: some are larger and some smaller.

The pendulum explains the sizes. Where the sun moves fastest, through the middle, a month covers a wide stretch of horizon, so those portals are large. Where it slows toward the ends, a month covers a narrow stretch, so those portals are small.

That gives us a ruler for measuring the rising and setting of the planets and stars along the sun’s path, the ecliptic, as seen from the Earth. A planet that rises in the second portal and later in the third has moved a measurable distance against a ruler everyone shares, and that is what makes the calendar an observatory: detailed celestial measurements, recorded and compared.

Try it in the Sky: click a planet or a star, and the app shows where it rises and sets against the portals.

8

Day and night

The portals also tell you how long the day is.

The day, from sunrise to sunrise, is divided into eighteen parts, and the portals carry a rule for how those parts are shared between light and dark. At the equinox, day and night are equal, nine parts each. As the sun moves north toward summer, the day grows until it is double the night, twelve parts to six. Toward winter it reverses: six parts of day to twelve of night.

The rule is not exact, since where you stand on the Earth decides the true ratio, but it describes how day and night behave through the year. The Evidence page shows where on Earth the text’s 2:1 is exact: between 47° and 50° N.

On the Dial, the ring of the 18 parts turns as the day goes by, so you can read the share of light and dark at any moment.

9

Cycles inside of cycles

The clock is cycles inside of cycles, and each one fits the next exactly.

Read each row from left to right. The weeks build the seasons and the year. The 24 priestly courses build six years, and 49 of those make 294 years. The correction climbs from 294 days to 294 weeks to 294 years. The priests and the correction arrive at the same 294.

On the Dial, the ring of the 24 courses shows each one as a week, with its seven days as the inner cells.

10

Where the 294 day shifts occur

Now you have everything you need to read the clock itself, and the Dial is where it all comes together.

On the Dial, every ring is turned so that its current value sits on the Sun’s line, which stays fixed. That is the rotation from section 4, made visible: the rings turn, and the Sun’s line reads each exact value.

The Correction ring counts the 294 days. 294 is 7 × 42, and 42 is 7 × 6, so the ring is built from weeks. A silver triangle marks the day each cycle opens, the day of the shift. Press 2 for the Dial, choose Day in the playback bar, and watch for the triangle: that is where a shift occurs.

Then press 1 to go back to the Sky and look at the same morning from the ground: the sun rising in its portal, and the planets and stars rising and setting beside it. Finding a rise or a setting in one view, and then in the other, is how you learn the clock.
