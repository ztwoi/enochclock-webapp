1

The claim

1.24days a year: how far a 364-day year falls behind the seasons

Enoch, Jubilees and the Qumran calendar scrolls hold that a year of 364 days — exactly 52 weeks — keeps time with the Sun and the seasons for ever (1 Enoch 74:12).

On its own it cannot. The seasons take 365.24 days; a 364-day year slips 1.24 days a year and walks all the way round the seasons in about 294 years.

Turn the calendar one day against the sun every 294 days and the slip disappears: it never strays a day from the seasons.

3

One in half a million

1 of 504,899designs that keep both the week and the seasons

Widen the search to every year from 300 to 400 days and every hold from every 2 days to every 5,000: 504,899 calendars.

Only 7 keep pace with the seasons to within half a day in a thousand years. Six of them are 365-day years with a rare hold about every 1,508 days.

One — and only one — is made entirely of whole weeks: 364 days, a day held every 294 (42 weeks). The next best whole-week calendar drifts 160 times faster.

The week and the seasons can be reconciled in exactly one way.

Thirty thousand years

15,850years in a row within a day and a half of the equinox

Every New Year's Day of the calendar from 12999 BCE to 16996 CE, measured against the true March equinox — 29,995 years, each computed from the Swiss Ephemeris.

Day 1 never strays further than 2.6 days early or 5.6 days late, and stays within a day and a half from 4261 BCE to 11589 CE.

The gold line is each century's average; the band is its earliest and latest New Year.

5

Why it holds

365.2415–365.2429days: the range the seasons' own year moves through

The seasons' year is not fixed. As the Earth's axis slowly turns against its orbit, the year from one March equinox to the next lengthens and shortens over about twenty thousand years.

The calendar's year — 365.24232 days — sits inside that range. When the seasons' year is shorter, Day 1 drifts one way; when it is longer, it drifts back.

So the calendar does not run away from the seasons: it swings around them. This is its true link to the precession of the Earth.

Where the text is the sky

47–50° Nwhere Enoch's longest and shortest days are measured fact

Enoch's longest day is twelve parts of light to six of darkness, and his shortest six to twelve.

Measured in the year 301 BCE, those proportions are true at 47.3° north (the longest day) and 50.1° north (the shortest). Jerusalem, at 31.8° north, sees about ten and a half parts at most.

R. H. Charles, in 1912, noted that the scheme may rest on traditions from 'the latitude of 49°, when the longest day is twice as long as the shortest night.'

The priestly beat

13turns of the 24 courses in six years — exactly

Twenty-four priestly families served a week each in turn. A year of 52 weeks does not divide by 24; six years do: 312 weeks, thirteen full turns.

Those same six years hold exactly 74 of the text's months. The true Moon falls 1.26 days short of that over six years — so the priestly cycle and the Moon keep step, give or take a day.

And 49 six-year cycles make up the calendar's great cycle of 294 years.

The first day

Wednesdaythe calendar's first day: the equinox, and a new moon

Counted back to its beginning, the calendar's first day falls on the March equinox, in the year 12763 BCE.

It is a Wednesday — the fourth day, the day on which, in Genesis, the lights were set in the heavens. The true new moon follows 1.15 days later.

The Sun, the Moon and the week begin together.
